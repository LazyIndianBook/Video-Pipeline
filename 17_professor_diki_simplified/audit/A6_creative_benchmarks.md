## 2. What we currently do that none of them do, and why that is a problem

From digest §2/§4 plus my calc [S59] on the three Opus `narration/timeline.json` files.

1. **Non-teaching beats take 14% of runtime.** First word at 0.7–0.8 s, a 5.1–5.4 s silent think gap and a 3.9–4.1 s spoken disclosure ("AI-generated narration; script by ExamLeaf") total 9.7–10.2 s of 67–73 s. No creator account I read has a spoken disclosure or publisher credit. It is our slowest line (75 wpm against 127–204 in teaching beats) and the last thing heard, where creators want the opening question resolved [S44].
2. **One unlabelled 5 s silence for every prompt.** Prediction evidence rewards commitment, not silence [S05,S06]; nothing supports 5 s inside 60 s.
3. **Teardown and per-video identity.** Board reset at 00:57; notebook, collage, sparse looks. The benchmarks keep one world per video and one grammar per channel, as far as sources show; ours makes viewers relearn colour and region meanings and breaks object constancy [S15,S16].
4. **Hooks that answer themselves or change subject.** "Did an atom just vanish?" half-answers the predict question; the banyan hook asks about wood, the payoff is carbon; physics opens on an empty board for 3–3.5 s. A gap must be specific and held [S29].
5. **A narrator with no behaviour.** The benchmarks are recognisable by what they do: misconception first, definitions last, drawn at speech pace, one slider at a time, no gimmicks. We have a stock voice (creators called robotic narration low-effort and disengaging [S44]), a stock "Pause and predict" and no repeated explanatory move. Pace is not the issue (138–149 wpm while teaching); dead time and the missing move are.

## 3. Humour and memes

Evidence: concept-related humour helps most consistently, chiefly mood and rapport, with some delayed retention; unrelated jokes act as seductive details (worse beside diagrams); hostile humour harms; the best frequency for video is unknown [S21,S30–S32]. Our pack's 3–5 moments in ~25 s suits a 10-minute lesson; in 60 s allow one, ≤4 s. Mould: the ah-ha outranks the ha-ha [S33]. A Sept 2026 Snapchat–Omnicom survey (13–28-year-olds, six countries incl. India; industry-commissioned) finds ~80% see jokey brands as “older people trying” and 79% say explaining a joke ruins it [S63].

**I could not verify any 2026 meme template as current and safe for Assam Class 10–12.** Verified signals argue against templates: "6-7" peaked in late 2025 and was being mocked by March 2026 [S51]; the largest 2026 youth meme movement I found, Cockroach Janta Party, is political satire tied to exam irregularities [S52,S53].

Usable patterns (made in-house, none template-bound):
1. Dry aside at the tempting shortcut, never the learner: "an efficient way to solve a different question".
2. Split-screen contrast as the meme: "changing the coefficient / changing the substance".
3. Deadpan result: the bent V–I line is the punchline ("the bulb disagreed").
4. Exam-friction aside ≤3 s on a graded step (unit, sign), or in perennial exam-room words ("sure-shot", "out of syllabus", "rough work"; unverified, reviewer to confirm); match the board scheme [S47].
5. "Doubt:" card in Indian-classroom words (doubt, numerical, marks); Pandey's frame is winning hearts and clearing doubts [S46].
6. Explainer self-deprecation ("my first drawing forgot the 2"): neutral for learning, warms rapport, rationed [S31,S63].
7. Familiar-object analogy with a wink (school gate at 1:30, phone charger, fan regulator), break point named [S26].
8. The pin that slips (§6): the gag is the lesson.

Anti-patterns:
1. Trend templates, trending audio, film or TV stills: they die within months and invite licensing and personality-rights claims; Delhi High Court gave interim protection to Aishwarya Rai Bachchan's name and likeness in Sept 2025 [S54].
2. Slang or in-group references as costume (bro, aura, 6-7) from an adult persona or synthetic voice; if a joke needs explaining, cut it [S63].
3. Political or protest satire; exam-leak edits.
4. Comparison and shame formats: "Sharma ji ka beta" (comparison pressure plus a surname that signals community), topper versus last-bencher, marks jokes. Use roles or objects ("Bulb A"); if names are wanted, the Assamese reviewer picks a short mixed set.
5. Unrelated cutaways or laugh tracks [S21].
6. Assumed Hindi or faux dialect from a synthetic voice: PW built its name on Hinglish batches yet now sells Hindi, English, Hinglish and nine regional editions [S64]; Assam's boards reportedly examine in six media [S50]; no controlled Hinglish evidence [S49]. Keep Indian-classroom English in the master; native reviewers adapt.

## 4. Sound identity

**Music.** Adding music and sounds to a narrated animation lowered retention and transfer [S22]; background music interferes with reading and slightly hurts memory while lifting mood [S23]; lyrics hurt most in the text's own language [S24]. Keep silence under teaching. Kurzgesagt's identity rests on commissioned music across minutes [S36]; Dave avoids songs [S39]; neither transfers.

**SFX.** Only on state changes that carry meaning: a tick when a pin is declared, a soft settle when the readout lands, a release when the pin slips. Silent during the prediction ring and while an equation is read. Each must be removable without losing meaning: feeds autoplay muted [S44]; I found no India sound-off data.

**End motif.** Practitioner audio logos run 1–3 s, 3–5 notes, with no research behind them [S58]. At 0.4–0.8 s ours is a sting, so recognition must come from repetition: 2–3 notes resolving upward, after the last word and final tick, never over a number. Test recognition after five exposures with students.

## 5. Phone-size craft checks

- **Type** (my calc [S60]; 6.1-inch phone ≈71 mm wide; assumed 35 cm; 20′ preferred, 16′ minimum character angle, ISO 9241 second-hand): 20′ ≈ 2.0 mm cap height ≈ 31 px ≈ 43 px font. Subscripts at 60% need a ≥72 px base; the 64 px token gives ≈18′.
- **Reveal:** motion onset, not motion itself, captures attention [S57]: crisp 180–300 ms onset, then stillness; no idle loops on key objects.
- **Captions:** keyword lane (Dave highlights keywords [S38]) plus a full caption track, since redundant text can help L2 viewers [S62]. ≤42 characters × 2 lines, ≤20 cps, so 6 words need ≥1.8 s [S55]. Unofficial ad-derived margins: 288 top, 672 bottom, 48 left, 192 right [S56]; Google publishes only an image template for ads [S65]; test on Shorts and Reels.
- **Mayer fit:** coherence = cut; signalling = colour and pin; contiguity = label touches object; segmenting = the three chapters (small effect [S20]); pre-training = name quantities first [S19].

## 6. Signature proposal: Pin, Turn, Release

Promise: this stays fixed, I change one thing, watch what answers, then see where it stops.

**Pin** (first 8 s): each held quantity is named, coloured and pinned: R = 10 Ω at constant temperature; the subscripts in H₂O. **Turn**: exactly one quantity moves in 400–900 ms steps (V 2→4→6 V; a coefficient 1→2) while one persistent readout answers (I 0.2→0.4→0.6 A; an atom tally). **Release**: once, the pin visibly slips (the filament heats and the line bends; a changed subscript turns water into peroxide) and the rule returns with its condition. **Mark**: the closing lines are the answer sheet, each ticked.

Why ours: it turns the variable-control habit that the Class 10 practical and “state the condition” questions already demand into a repeated grammar, and it travels: physics, chemistry (subscripts pinned, coefficients turned), biology (carbon pinned, form turned). I found no benchmark making it a visible, repeated device.

Rules: pin only what is truly held; one turn; one release. Sound is the pin tick plus the end motif. The script ledger exempts this grammar from its repetition check; openings, analogies and jokes still vary. QA fails a short with no pin, two turns or no release.
